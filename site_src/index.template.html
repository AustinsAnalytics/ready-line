00 · Bottom line up front

A model buys readiness. A better model mostly buys money.

  1. Engines wear out, so timing matters. A Weibull fit to {{hypotheses.h1.groups.2.n|int}} run-to-failure engines gives shape β = {{hypotheses.h1.groups.2.beta|f2}} (95% CI {{hypotheses.h1.groups.2.beta_lo|f2}}–{{hypotheses.h1.groups.2.beta_hi|f2}}). The "failures are random" hypothesis (β = 1) is rejected, p {{hypotheses.h1.groups.2.p|p}}.
  2. The removal can be predicted. A LightGBM + 1D-CNN ensemble predicts remaining useful life on NASA's withheld test engines to within {{d.rmse_fd001|f1}} cycles RMSE (FD001) and {{d.rmse_dz|f1}} cycles in the decision zone, with calibrated 80% intervals.
  3. The prediction moves readiness. Replaying the model's real predictions inside a GPU Monte Carlo of a 24-jet squadron lifts the mission-capable rate from {{d.mc_base|pct1}} to {{d.mc_pred|pct1}} and cuts unscheduled engine removals from {{d.un_base|f1}} to {{d.un_pred|f1}} a year. Most of the gain comes from shorter depot visits for engines pulled before they break.
  4. Past "good enough," accuracy buys dollars, not sorties. Scaling the model's error from zero (perfect foresight) to four times its real size moves readiness by only {{d.vop_mc_spread|f1}} points but adds ${{d.vop_cost_spread|f1}}M a year in early removals. A ridge regression with {{d.ridge_err_ratio|f1}}× the ensemble's decision-zone error still reaches {{d.ridge_mc|pct1}} MC. What matters most is having a warning at all.
  5. The spares plan should change with the policy. With predictive removal, the optimal spares mix needs {{d.eng_drop_txt}} for the same availability, and an exact MILP matches a common stocking rule's availability for {{d.rot_saving|pct0}} less money.

01 · Problem

Readiness is falling, and spares and depot speed are the named causes

The Government Accountability Office reported in June 2026 that the F-35 fleet's mission-capable rate fell from 67% in fiscal 2021 to 44% in fiscal 2025, and its full-mission-capable rate from 38% to 25%. The program's updated sustainment strategy aims for 80% and 65% by 2030.

F-35As without an engine during the 2021 F135 depot backlog (HASC hearing, USNI News)

GAO and the program office trace low sustainment performance to insufficient spares, depot repair speed and the ability to buy parts. Engines show how these interact. When engines fail unexpectedly, they arrive at the depot with more damage, take longer to repair, and empty the spare pool. Then healthy airframes sit waiting.

Problem statement

Given a squadron's engines, their sensor histories and the fleet's maintenance records, decide when each engine should come off the wing and what spares and depot capacity to hold, so the squadron meets a {{simulation.pbl_target|pct0}} mission-capable requirement at the lowest sustainment cost.

03 · Discover

The degradation signal is half a percent of the variance

In the multi-condition subsets, the flight regime explains {{explore.regimes.noise.regime_r2|pct1}} of the variance in a key sensor. Degradation hides in what's left.

Which sensors carry the signal

I scored all 21 sensors on three prognostic criteria: monotonicity (does it move one way?), trendability (does it track age?) and prognosability (do engines fail at a similar value?). Seven sensors are flat or nearly flat and carry no degradation information. The top five are temperatures and pressures around the high-pressure compressor, which is where C-MAPSS injects its main fault.

A hidden second fault mode, found without labels

NASA documents that FD003 and FD004 contain two fault modes, but it does not say which engine has which. For each engine I computed an end-of-life signature: its last 20 cycles minus its first 30, across 14 sensors. I then asked a Gaussian mixture model how many groups the signatures form.

SubsetDocumented fault modesΔBIC, 1 → 2 groupsDip test p (H₀: unimodal)Conclusion

The data recover the documentation exactly: one mode in FD001 and FD002, two in FD003 and FD004. The second mode reverses the sign of the coolant-bleed (W31, W32) and bypass-ratio (BPR) signatures, which is consistent with fan degradation. It also matters: engines in that mode lasted {{d.mode_life_b|f0}} cycles on average versus {{d.mode_life_a|f0}}. That gap is tested formally as H3.

04 · Test

Five hypotheses, five decisions

Each hypothesis was stated before testing, with a significance level of 0.05 and the decision it would change. Two of them shaped the simulator, and one came back as a useful null.

05 · Predict

Remaining useful life to within a few cycles where it counts

The models learn from 160,359 engine-cycles. They never see an engine in both training and validation, and they are scored once on NASA's 707 withheld test engines.

Features are causal by construction: trailing rolling means, volatility, 30-cycle slopes, and each sensor's deviation from the engine's own early-life baseline, which turned out to be the most important feature family. The target is RUL capped at {{models.cap|int}} cycles, the standard piecewise-linear treatment, because early-life engines carry no degradation signal.

ModelFD001FD002FD003FD004All 707Decision zonetrue RUL ≤ 60NASA score

RMSE in cycles against NASA's true RUL at each test engine's last observed cycle; lower is better. The NASA score penalizes late predictions more than early ones. The ensemble weight ({{models.ensemble_weight|f2}} LightGBM) was chosen on out-of-fold predictions only. For context, earlier deep-learning benchmarks tabulated in arXiv:1810.05644 report 18.45 / 30.29 / 19.82 / 29.16 for a CNN and 16.14 / 24.49 / 16.18 / 28.17 for an LSTM. More recent architectures report lower errors.

Intervals come from split-conformal prediction: out-of-fold residuals, grouped by operating profile and predicted-RUL band, set each band's width. Across all test cycles the 80% intervals cover {{d.cov80_all|pct1}} of the truth. In the decision zone they cover close to their nominal rate. They are over-confident only above 100 cycles, where both the model and the truth sit at the cap. The alert classifier separates engines inside their last 30 cycles with ROC AUC {{models.alert.auc_all|f3}}.

GPU training

The CNN trained on the Apple M2 GPU through PyTorch's MPS backend: {{gpu.epoch_seconds.mps|f1}} s per epoch versus {{gpu.epoch_seconds.cpu|f1}} s on the CPU for the same workload, {{d.gpu_speedup|f1}}× faster. Windows are gathered on the device from one padded tensor, so 160,000 30-cycle windows never get copied into memory.

06 · Read the logs

What 624,000 maintenance write-ups say actually breaks

Sensor models say when. Mechanics' write-ups say what and why. Text mining turns the free-text column of the SDR database into structured evidence for root-cause analysis.

Auto-coding write-ups to the right system

Reliability analytics depend on every report carrying the right system code. I trained a classifier to read a write-up and predict its JASC chapter (one of {{nlp.classifier.n_classes|int}} systems), training on 2016–2023 and testing on 2024–2025, as it would run in production. Write-ups often cite maintenance-manual references like IAW AMM 33-51-38, which literally contain the chapter number. I masked every reference to check that the model reads the language rather than the citations.

Reconciling the codes

When the model is at least 90% confident and still disagrees with the coded system, the record is worth a second look. That flagged {{nlp.classifier.miscode.n|int}} of {{nlp.classifier.miscode.n_test|int}} reports ({{nlp.classifier.miscode.rate|pct1}}) for review. Some are real coding errors; others are reports that touch two systems. Either way, an analyst now reviews {{nlp.classifier.miscode.n|int}} records instead of {{nlp.classifier.miscode.n_test|int}}.

Failure modes that interrupt flights

For the {{nlp.topics.n|int}} engine-system reports, a non-negative matrix factorization topic model found {{nlp.topics.k|int}} recurring failure themes. I named each one after reading its top terms and example write-ups. Ranking themes by volume times the share that ended in a precautionary procedure (unscheduled landing, engine shutdown, aborted take-off) gives a risk-weighted Pareto. That ranking is the right order for root-cause work.

07 · Forecast

Demand for engine parts: forecast the fleet, smooth the parts

Spares decisions need demand forecasts. Engine "remove and replace" actions in the SDR data serve as a demand signal: fleet-wide totals are smooth and seasonal, while individual parts are intermittent.

Model (holdout 2024–25)MASERMSE

MASE below 1 beats a seasonal naive forecast's in-sample error. The SARIMAX model includes a COVID-19 intervention regressor, informed by H5.

For {{forecast.parts.n_series|int}} individual engine parts, demand is mostly intermittent: many zero months, then a few replacements. Croston, SBA and TSB are the standard methods for that pattern. On a 2025 holdout they beat the naive forecast by about 15% on RMSSE, the M5-competition metric. A 12-month moving average did just as well. The practical answer is to use the simplest method that matches the specialists.

08 · Simulate

Turning prediction error into mission-capable rate

A model's RMSE doesn't fly sorties. To translate accuracy into readiness, I built a Monte Carlo simulator of a notional squadron. It replays the model's actual predictions for real engines and runs thousands of squadron-years at once on the GPU.

How one simulated day works

  1. Repaired engines return from the depot to the shelf.
  2. Jets waiting on an engine take a spare if one is on the shelf.
  3. Each mission-capable jet flies with probability {{simulation.base.p_fly|f2}}; its engine ages one cycle.
  4. An engine that reaches its life fails: an unscheduled removal and a {{simulation.base.tat_unsched|f0}}-day depot visit.
  5. Under predictive removal, an engine is pulled when the model predicts ≤ {{d.th|int}} cycles left, or up to {{simulation.base.opportunistic|int}} cycles earlier if a spare is already on the shelf. It gets a {{simulation.base.tat_planned|f0}}-day visit.
  6. Other systems break at the calibrated rate and ground the jet for maintenance or parts.

Why replay real predictions

Each simulated engine is one of the 709 real training engines: its true life and the ensemble's out-of-fold prediction at every cycle. That means the simulator inherits the model's real error pattern, including its occasional late call. Scaling that error up or down shows what a better or worse model would be worth.

Every policy threshold was tuned on one random stream and scored on another, so the comparison isn't flattered by its own tuning.

PolicyMC rateP(MC ≥ {{simulation.pbl_target|pct0}})Unscheduled / yrPlanned / yrEngine life usedCost / yr

Hard-time removal, the textbook answer to wear-out, cuts cost but not readiness. It pulls healthy engines and throws away about {{d.hard_waste|pct0}} of their life. The extra removals load the depot, so the squadron ends at {{d.hard_mc|pct1}} MC, slightly below run to failure. Predictive removal uses {{d.pred_life_used|pct0}} of each engine's life, avoids almost every failure, and lands within {{d.oracle_gap|f1}} points of a perfect-foresight oracle. A ridge regression gets {{d.ridge_mc|pct1}}: readiness depends on having a warning at all, and accuracy shows up mostly in cost.

09 · Optimize

Buy the spares that buy availability

With demand and turnaround in hand, the question becomes prescriptive. Which stock levels, across the engine and its line-replaceable units (LRUs), give the most aircraft availability per dollar?

The model follows the METRIC family used across military supply: each item's repair pipeline is Poisson (Palm's theorem), stock reduces expected backorders, and availability is the product over items of the chance an aircraft isn't waiting on that item. Choosing one stock level per item under a budget is a multiple-choice knapsack. I solved it exactly as a mixed-integer program in Pyomo with the open-source HiGHS solver; Gurobi or CPLEX plug in by changing one string. The relative demand for 12 engine LRUs comes from real SDR replacement counts. Engine demand and turnaround come from the simulator under each policy.

Two results stand out. First, predictive removal shrinks the engine pipeline, so the optimizer needs {{d.eng_drop_txt}} to reach the same availability and moves money into cheaper LRUs. Second, a common rule of thumb (stock every item at its mean pipeline, rounded up) costs ${{d.rot_cost|f1}}M for {{d.rot_A|pct1}} availability. The MILP matches that availability for ${{d.rot_milp|f1}}M, {{d.rot_saving|pct0}} less. As a cross-check, classic greedy marginal analysis never beats the MILP and trails it by at most {{d.greedy_gap|pts1}} availability points at any budget.

The textbook replacement age, and why the depot changes it

For a single engine with Weibull wear-out, the cost-optimal replacement age T* minimizes the long-run cost rate C(T) = [CpR(T) + CfF(T)] / ∫0T R(t) dt. Using the fitted 3-parameter Weibull (failure-free period γ = {{hypotheses.h1.groups.2.w3.gamma|f0}} cycles), the math says to replace early. It assumes spares and depot capacity are unlimited. The simulator shows what happens when they aren't.

Which engines go to the depot, and when

The NASA test sets are a snapshot of engines in service, with histories cut off at an unknown point before failure. I treated the {{optimization.induction.n|int}} FD001 and FD003 test engines as one fleet and scheduled their depot inductions over the next {{optimization.induction.params.weeks|int}} weeks ({{optimization.induction.params.cycles_per_week|int}} sorties a week). Constraints: {{optimization.induction.params.cap|int}} depot slots a week, {{optimization.induction.params.spares0|int}} spare engines now and {{optimization.induction.params.returns_per_week|f0}} returning each week. Each engine's failure risk comes from the calibrated out-of-fold error distribution, never from the test answers.

11 · Limits & next steps

What would change with real program data

Limits

  • C-MAPSS is simulated. Real engines get interventions, sensors drop out, and many histories end in a removal for other reasons. That calls for survival models with censoring (Cox, Weibull AFT) rather than clean run-to-failure labels.
  • SDRs are commercial and voluntary. They show what fails and what it does to a flight, but rates are biased by who reports. I used them for relative mix, never absolute rates.
  • Costs and turnarounds are notional. The sensitivity analysis shows the predictive-removal benefit shrinks sharply if planned depot visits are not shorter than unscheduled ones. That is the first number to measure on a real program.
  • METRIC is single-echelon. It assumes Poisson demand and ignores lateral resupply between bases. The simulator covers depot capacity limits that METRIC ignores.

Next steps

  • Retrain on N-CMAPSS, NASA's 2021 dataset with real flight profiles, at full scale on Spark.
  • Bayesian RUL updating as each new cycle arrives, so the interval narrows in operation.
  • Multi-echelon spares (VARI-METRIC) with the simulator as a validation harness.
  • LLM-assisted triage of write-ups into structured component, failure-mode and action fields, with an evaluation set built from the classifier's agreements and the miscode queue.
  • Shadow-mode pilot: score live engines for a quarter without acting, then compare predicted and actual removals.
